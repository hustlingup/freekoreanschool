Stage 1 · Batchim 받침

What is Batchim? 받침이란?

A batchim (받침, literally 'support') is a consonant placed at the bottom of a Korean syllable block. For example, in the syllable 강, the ㅇ at the bottom is the batchim. Not all syllables have a batchim — many end with just the vowel. Batchim is why Korean words like 산, 달, and 밥 end with a distinct consonant sound rather than an open vowel.

🔷 Which syllables have batchim?

Look at any Korean syllable block. If there is a character beneath the vowel, that is the batchim. 가 has no batchim. 간 has batchim ㄴ. 닭 has double batchim ㄺ (read as one sound).

The 7 Batchim Sound Groups 받침 7종성

Although many different consonants can appear as batchim in spelling, they all reduce to only 7 possible sounds when pronounced. This is known as the 7 rules of batchim (받침 7종성). Learning these 7 groups is essential — it explains why 닭 (chicken) and 국 (soup) both end with the same k sound.

  • ㄱ-group: ㄱ, ㄲ, ㅋ, ㄳ, ㄺ → pronounced k (unreleased) — example: 국 (soup), 닭 (chicken)
  • ㄴ-group: ㄴ, ㄵ, ㄶ → pronounced n — example: 산 (mountain), 앉다 (to sit)
  • ㄷ-group: ㄷ, ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅌ, ㅎ → pronounced t (unreleased) — example: 옷 (clothes), 꽃 (flower)
  • ㄹ-group: ㄹ, ㄼ, ㄽ, ㄾ, ㅀ → pronounced l — example: 달 (moon), 말 (horse)
  • ㅁ-group: ㅁ, ㄻ → pronounced m — example: 밤 (night), 삶 (life)
  • ㅂ-group: ㅂ, ㅍ, ㄿ, ㄼ → pronounced p (unreleased) — example: 입 (mouth), 앞 (front)
  • ㅇ-group: ㅇ → pronounced ng — example: 강 (river), 방 (room)

💡 Unreleased stops — what does that mean?

The k, t, and p batchim sounds are unreleased: your mouth forms the position but does not release a puff of air. Think of holding the final p in 'cup' — cup_ — without letting air out. Korean ㄱ/ㄷ/ㅂ batchim work the same way.

Stage 2 · Liaison 연음화

Liaison — Linking Sound 연음화

When a syllable ending in a batchim is immediately followed by a syllable that begins with the silent ㅇ, the batchim consonant moves forward and becomes the initial consonant of the next syllable. The spelling stays the same — only the pronunciation shifts. This is called liaison (연음화) and it is one of the most frequent pronunciation phenomena in Korean.

🔗 Spot the pattern

Look for: batchim consonant + next syllable beginning with ㅇ (silent placeholder). Result: the batchim sounds as if it opens the next syllable. 먹어요 → 머거요. 한국어 → 한구거. The written form never changes — only your pronunciation does.

Words and phrases

KoreanPronunciationMeaning
먹어요meo-geo-yoI eat / am eating — ㄱ batchim of 먹 links into 어, sounds like 머거요
밥을ba-beulrice (object form) — ㅂ batchim of 밥 links into 을, sounds like 바블
좋아요jo-a-yoIt is good / I like it — ㅎ batchim weakens and links (see Stage 4 for ㅎ rules)
한국어han-gu-geoKorean language — ㄱ batchim of 국 links into 어, sounds like 한구거

Why Does Liaison Happen? 왜 연음화가 일어날까?

Korean syllable structure strongly prefers the pattern consonant + vowel. When a vowel-initial syllable follows a batchim, it is phonetically easier for the consonant to attach to that vowel's open slot. The result is a more natural, connected flow of speech. This is not slang or lazy pronunciation — it is standard Korean and will be heard in news broadcasts, formal speeches, and everyday conversation alike.

ℹ️ Liaison vs spelling

Written Korean preserves the original consonant in its spelling position even when it is pronounced in the next syllable. This is why learning to read Korean and learning to pronounce Korean are two separate skills at first — the script records morphemes, not exact sounds.

Answer: 한구거 (한구거 · 한국어 · 한구어 · 항구거)

Stage 3 · Assimilation 비음화

Nasal Assimilation 비음화

When a stop consonant batchim from the ㄱ, ㄷ, or ㅂ groups is followed by the nasal consonants ㄴ or ㅁ, the stop assimilates and changes to its corresponding nasal sound. This is purely articulatory: nasal consonants require the velum to lower, which pulls adjacent stops into nasal territory. The spelling stays the same; only the pronunciation changes.

  • ㅂ + ㄴ/ㅁ → ㅁ: the lip-stop becomes a lip-nasal — 입니다 → 임니다
  • ㄱ + ㄴ/ㅁ → ㅇ: the velar-stop becomes a velar-nasal — 국물 → 궁물
  • ㄷ + ㄴ/ㅁ → ㄴ: the alveolar-stop becomes an alveolar-nasal — 걷는다 → 건는다

💡 Why the same place of articulation?

Each pair (ㅂ↔ㅁ, ㄱ↔ㅇ, ㄷ↔ㄴ) shares exactly the same place of articulation in the mouth — lips, throat back, and tooth ridge respectively. Only the nasal airflow changes. The nasal consonant 'infects' the preceding stop, pulling it nasal while keeping the mouth position.

Words and phrases

국물gung-mulbroth — ㄱ + ㅁ → ㅇ: spelled 국물, sounds like 궁물
학년hang-nyeonschool year / grade — ㄱ + ㄴ → ㅇ: spelled 학년, sounds like 항년

The Assimilation Pattern is Always One Direction 동화 방향

Nasal assimilation always goes the same way: the stop becomes nasal, never the other way around. The nasal consonant 'infects' the preceding sound. Once you spot the pattern ㅂ→ㅁ, ㄷ→ㄴ, ㄱ→ㅇ, you will start hearing and predicting these changes automatically in real Korean speech.

🧠 Memory trick

Think of each pair as sharing a location in your mouth. Lips: ㅂ (stop) ↔ ㅁ (nasal). Throat: ㄱ (stop) ↔ ㅇ (nasal). Teeth ridge: ㄷ (stop) ↔ ㄴ (nasal). The location stays; only the nasal valve opens.

Stage 4 · Tensification 경음화

Tensification 경음화

After certain batchim consonants — specifically the unreleased stop batchim sounds from the ㄱ, ㄷ, and ㅂ groups — the following consonant becomes tensed (doubled). The written form does not change; only the pronunciation shifts. Tensification is why 학교 (school) sounds like 학꾜 instead of 학교.

ℹ️ Why tensification happens

After an unreleased stop, the vocal tract is already in a tense, closed position. When you start the next consonant from this state, extra muscular tension carries over, creating the tensed sound automatically. Tensification is not deliberate — it is a natural articulatory consequence. Once you hear it in real speech, you cannot unhear it.

Words and phrases

KoreanPronunciationMeaning
학교hak-kkyoschool — ㄱ batchim triggers tensification: ㄱ→ㄲ, sounds like 학꾜
식당sik-ttangrestaurant — ㄱ batchim triggers tensification: ㄷ→ㄸ, sounds like 식땅
닫다dat-ttato close — ㄷ batchim triggers tensification: ㄷ→ㄸ, sounds like 닫따
입구ip-kkuentrance — ㅂ batchim triggers tensification: ㄱ→ㄲ, sounds like 입꾸

ㅎ Weakening ㅎ 약화

The consonant ㅎ is one of Korean's most unstable sounds. Between vowels — whether ㅎ is a batchim or an initial consonant — it weakens significantly and often disappears almost entirely. This is why 좋아요 (it is good) sounds like 조아요 rather than 조하요. The ㅎ is still written, but the sound nearly vanishes.

💡 Common beginner error

Many beginners say 조하요 for 좋아요, preserving the h sound. In real Korean speech this sounds unnatural and overly deliberate. The standard pronunciation is 조아요 — the ㅎ silently disappears between the two vowels. Trust the rule: ㅎ between vowels nearly vanishes.

Words and phrases

KoreanPronunciationMeaning
좋아요jo-a-yoIt is good / I like it — ㅎ batchim weakens: sounds like 조아요 (not 조하요)
많아요ma-na-yoThere is a lot — ㅎ in ㄶ batchim weakens: sounds like 마나요
넣어요neo-eo-yoI put it in — ㅎ batchim weakens between vowels: sounds like 너어요

ㅎ + Consonant = Aspiration ㅎ 축약

When ㅎ batchim meets the next syllable's initial consonant (or vice versa), the two merge into a single aspirated consonant. ㅎ + ㄷ becomes ㅌ. ㄱ + ㅎ becomes ㅋ. ㅂ + ㅎ becomes ㅍ. ㄷ + ㅎ (or ㅎ + ㄷ) becomes ㅌ. Think of ㅎ as adding a puff of air to the adjacent consonant.

  • ㅎ + ㄷ → ㅌ: 놓다 (to place) → 노타
  • ㄱ + ㅎ → ㅋ: 착하다 (kind) → 차카다
  • ㅂ + ㅎ → ㅍ: 입학 (enrollment) → 이팍
  • ㄷ + ㅎ → ㅌ: 못해요 (can't do) → 모태요

Words and phrases

KoreanPronunciationMeaning
놓다no-tato let go / to place — ㅎ batchim + ㄷ → ㅌ: spelled 놓다, sounds like 노타

Check your understanding

What sound does ㅎ + ㄷ (or ㄷ + ㅎ) produce?
Answer: ㅌ (aspirated t) (ㄷ (plain) · ㅌ (aspirated t) · ㄸ (tensed) · ㅎ (stays h))

Stage 5 · Palatal & ㄹ 구개음화

Palatalization 구개음화

When the consonants ㄷ or ㅌ appear as batchim and are followed by the vowel 이 (i), they shift forward in the mouth and change into ㅈ and ㅊ respectively. This shift is called palatalization — the consonant moves from the tooth ridge to the palate to anticipate the front vowel 이.

  • ㄷ + 이 → ㅈ이 → 지: 굳이 (deliberately) → 구지
  • ㅌ + 이 → ㅊ이 → 치: 같이 (together) → 가치

💡 Morpheme-internal only

Palatalization applies only within the same word or when a suffix beginning with 이 attaches to the stem. It does not apply across word boundaries. 같이 triggers palatalization because 이 is part of the word. In a phrase like 옷 입어요 (putting on clothes), 이 starts a separate word — different rules apply.

Words and phrases

KoreanPronunciationMeaning
같이ga-chitogether — ㅌ + 이 → ㅊ: spelled 같이, sounds like 가치
굳이gu-jideliberately / stubbornly — ㄷ + 이 → ㅈ: spelled 굳이, sounds like 구지

Check your understanding

When ㄷ batchim is followed by the vowel 이, it changes to…
Answer: ㅈ (ㄴ · ㅈ · ㅊ · ㅅ)
When ㅌ batchim is followed by the vowel 이, it changes to…
Answer: ㅊ (ㄴ · ㅈ · ㅊ · ㅅ)

The ㄹ Sound 리을

The Korean consonant ㄹ is often described as between r and l — and that is precisely correct. Its exact realisation depends on its position within the syllable. Between two vowels, ㄹ is a single fast tap of the tongue tip against the ridge just behind the upper teeth — the same motion as the flap r in American English 'butter' or 'water'. At the end of a syllable or before a consonant, hold the tongue lightly at that same ridge for a gentle l sound.

💡 Tongue-tap technique

Do not roll ㄹ (no Spanish trill) and do not use an English l-shape in vowel position. In 라면 the ㄹ is a quick tap — try saying 'la' while thinking 'ra'. In 달 (moon) the ㄹ is a held l. In 빨리 (quickly) you get both: a held l then a tap.

Words and phrases

KoreanPronunciationMeaning
라면ra-myeoninstant noodles — ㄹ in initial position (before vowel): quick r-tap
달dalmoon — ㄹ as final batchim: held l sound
빨리ppal-liquickly, fast — doubled ㄹㄹ: held l then r-tap
사랑해요sa-rang-hae-yoI love you — ㄹ between vowels (랑→해): r-tap sound

ㄹ + ㄴ or ㄴ + ㄹ → ㄹㄹ 유음화

When ㄹ and ㄴ appear adjacent across syllables, they both become ㄹ — this is called lateralisation (유음화). Example: 신라 (Silla dynasty) is pronounced 실라 (silla), not sin-ra. Similarly, 연락 (contact) is pronounced 열락 (yeollak). The ㄴ fully converts to ㄹ when adjacent to ㄹ.

ℹ️ Why lateralisation?

ㄹ and ㄴ share very similar tongue positions — both are alveolar sounds made at the tooth ridge. When they appear next to each other, the stronger (lateral) ㄹ pulls the ㄴ into its territory. This is one of the most striking sound changes because the spelling gives no visual hint it is happening.

Check your understanding

신라 (Silla — a historical Korean kingdom) is pronounced as…
Answer: sil-la (sin-ra · sin-la · sil-la · shi-ra)

Stage 6 · Mistakes 흔한 실수

6 Common Mistakes for English Speakers 흔한 발음 실수

Korean and English have very different phonological systems. The next six steps each cover one common pronunciation error made by English speakers — with clear explanations of how to correct it. Recognising these patterns early saves months of bad habits.

🎯 What makes these so common?

English speakers automatically apply English phonology to new sounds. Korean has vowels, consonant contrasts, and intonation patterns that simply do not exist in English. Each of these mistakes comes from mapping a Korean sound onto the nearest English equivalent — which is almost always wrong.

Mistake 1 — Pronouncing ㅡ like 'oo' ㅡ 발음 오류

ㅡ has no English equivalent. It is a back unrounded vowel — your tongue is in the oo position but your lips are completely flat and unrounded, as if you are saying 'uh' with a stiff, spread mouth. Words like 으, 크다, 든지 all use this sound. The moment your lips round, you are producing the wrong vowel.

💡 How to practise ㅡ

Say 'oo' as in 'too'. Now keep your tongue in exactly that position but spread your lips flat as if smiling slightly. The sound that comes out — stiff, unrounded, slightly back — is ㅡ. Do not relax the tongue into an 'uh' or a schwa; keep it high and back.

Mistake 2 — Treating ㅓ as English 'er' ㅓ 발음 오류

ㅓ is often transcribed as 'eo' or 'uh' — but it is not the American English 'er' which carries an r-colouring. Korean ㅓ is a mid-back unrounded vowel. Think of the British English 'uh' in 'but' or 'cup' — no rounding, no r-sound, mouth slightly open. Adding any hint of r-sound immediately makes the vowel sound foreign.

💡 Quick test

Say the name 'Uh-oh'. The first syllable 'Uh' — flat, mid-back, with no r and no lip rounding — is very close to ㅓ. Do not tense it, do not round it, do not add r. Just a plain open 'uh'. Words to practise: 어, 어머니, 뭐.

Mistake 3 — Puffing Air on Double Consonants 된소리 오류

English speakers naturally add aspiration to stop consonants. Korean tensed (doubled) consonants ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ, ㅆ, ㅉ are never aspirated. They are produced with tight muscles and no outward puff of air. Hold a piece of paper in front of your mouth — it should not move when you say 까, 따, 빠, 싸, 짜. The contrast is between tight-and-held versus pushed-out air.

💡 The paper test

Aspirated consonants (ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ, ㅊ) make the paper flutter significantly. Plain consonants (ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅈ) make it move slightly. Tensed consonants (ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ, ㅉ) should make the paper barely move at all — high muscle tension, zero air release.

Mistake 4 — Rising Intonation on All Questions 억양 오류

In English, rising intonation at the end of a sentence signals a question. In Korean, intonation rules differ: yes/no questions do use a slight rise at the end, but wh-questions (who, what, where, when, why, how) typically use a falling or neutral intonation — not rising. Overusing English-style rising intonation on all Korean questions sounds unnatural and sometimes uncertain or pleading.

ℹ️ The pattern to remember

Yes/No question: slight rise at the end. Example: 갔어요? (Did you go?) — ends rising. Wh-question: neutral or falling. Example: 어디 갔어요? (Where did you go?) — ends flat or falling. The question word carries enough information; no need for the rising melody.

Mistake 5 — ㅅ as Plain 's' before i-Vowels ㅅ 발음 오류

Before the vowels 이, 야, 여, 요, 유, the consonant ㅅ is pronounced like English 'sh'. So 시 is 'shi' not 'si', and 셔츠 (shirt) begins with 'sh'. This also applies to tensed ㅆ before 이 → 'sshi'. English speakers who learned romanization first often say 'si' when native speakers say 'shi', which is immediately noticeable.

💡 Which vowels trigger this?

The palatalised ㅅ ('sh' sound) occurs before i-class vowels: 이 (i), 야 (ya), 여 (yeo), 요 (yo), 유 (yu). Before all other vowels — 아, 어, 오, 우, 으, and their compounds — ㅅ stays as a plain s sound. 사 is 'sa', but 시 is 'shi'.

Mistake 6 — Pronouncing the ㅎ in 좋아요 ㅎ 오류

Beginners often say 'jo-ha-yo' for 좋아요, treating ㅎ as a clearly pronounced h-sound. But due to ㅎ weakening between vowels (which you learned in Stage 4), the actual pronunciation is 조아요 (jo-a-yo) — the ㅎ nearly disappears. This applies broadly: 많아요 → 마나요, 낳아요 → 나아요. Whenever ㅎ sits between vowels in a natural speech context, it fades.

💡 Connecting back to Stage 4

You already learned ㅎ weakening and ㅎ + consonant aspiration. Mistake 6 is just the most common real-world place where beginners encounter ㅎ weakening and get it wrong. 좋아요 is arguably the most-used adjective form in Korean — getting its pronunciation right matters immediately.
