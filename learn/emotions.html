Expressing Emotions — 감정 표현

Read the lesson, then practise at your own pace. All explanations and examples are on this page.

Core Emotions 기본 감정

Emotions in Korean (감정)

Korean emotion words are mostly adjectives in their base form. Unlike English ('I am happy'), Korean adjectives act like verbs and conjugate directly: 행복해요 (I am happy) without needing a separate 'am'. The dictionary form ends in -다: 행복하다, 슬프다, 무섭다. Drop -다 and add -아요/-어요 for the polite present tense. Emotion vocabulary is especially useful for talking about K-dramas, daily life, and personal feelings.

Adjectives conjugate like verbs

행복하다 → 행복해요 (I am happy). 슬프다 → 슬퍼요 (I am sad). 피곤하다 → 피곤해요 (I am tired). The -아요/-어요 ending makes the adjective polite and present tense simultaneously.

Conjugating Emotion Adjectives 감정 형용사 활용

To use emotion adjectives in polite conversation, remove -다 from the dictionary form and add the polite present ending. For stems ending in 하: 하다 → 해요 (행복하다 → 행복해요). For stems ending in a bright vowel (ㅏ, ㅗ): add -아요. For all others: add -어요, usually with a vowel change — 슬프다 drops its ㅡ: 슬프 + 어요 → 슬퍼요. Stems ending in ㅂ are irregular: the ㅂ turns into 우, so 무섭다 → 무서우 + 어요 → 무서워요 (likewise 외롭다 → 외로워요, 부끄럽다 → 부끄러워요). The same pattern applies to most Korean descriptive verbs (adjectives).

Quick conjugation chart

행복하다 → 행복해요. 슬프다 → 슬퍼요. 화나다 → 화나요. 무섭다 → 무서워요. 피곤하다 → 피곤해요. 기쁘다 → 기뻐요. Note that 슬프다 drops the 으 before -어요.

Expressing Feelings 감정 표현

More Feeling Words (감정 어휘)

Korean has a rich set of emotion words beyond the basics. 기쁘다 (to be glad/joyful) is a slightly stronger or more specific happiness than 행복하다. 걱정되다 (to be worried) literally means 'worry becomes/happens'. 신나다 (to be excited/pumped) is commonly used for energetic situations — concerts, sports, celebrations. 피곤하다 (to be tired) is essential for everyday life, especially when explaining you can't do something.

신나다 in pop culture

신나다 and 신나요 are everywhere in Korean pop culture. K-pop songs often use 신나 to describe an exciting beat or mood. You'll also hear 신난다! as an exclamation (This is so exciting!) at events and parties.

기분 — Mood & Feeling

기분 (gibun) is the key word for describing your mood or general feeling state. 기분이 좋다 (gibuni jota) = to feel good / to be in a good mood. 기분이 나쁘다 = to feel bad / to be in a bad mood. 기분 can describe emotional tone more broadly than single-emotion words — it's about your overall state. You'll hear 기분이 어때요? (How are you feeling?) in everyday conversation.

기분 vs 감정

기분 = mood, general feeling state (contextual, can change). 감정 = emotion (more specific feeling like anger, joy, fear). 기분이 좋아요 = I feel good right now. 감정을 표현하다 = to express emotion.

Complex Emotions 복잡한 감정

Deeper Emotions (깊은 감정)

Korean has several emotion words with no single-word English equivalent. 그립다 (geuripda) describes missing someone or something — the longing for an absent person or place you love. 외롭다 (oeropda) is loneliness, but with a cultural depth of isolation. 부끄럽다 (bukkeureupda) is embarrassment or shyness. 뿌듯하다 (ppudeuthada) is the warm pride or satisfaction of accomplishment — the feeling you get when you finish something hard or watch someone you care about succeed.

한 (Han) — untranslatable Korean sorrow

한 (han) is a culturally specific emotion: a deep sorrow mixed with resilience, rooted in historical suffering but turned into creative energy. It's heard in pansori (Korean folk opera), described in poetry, and referenced in discussions of Korean identity. Not a word to use casually, but essential for cultural understanding.

Emotional Phrases 감정 문장

Common Emotional Sentence Patterns 감정 표현 문장

Several sentence patterns are especially useful for expressing emotions in Korean. '보고 싶어요' (I miss you / I want to see you) uses 보다 (to see) + -고 싶다 (to want to). '감동받았어요' (I was moved/touched) uses 감동 (deep emotion) + 받다 (to receive). '괜찮아요' (I'm okay / That's fine) is one of the most versatile phrases — used for 'I'm fine', 'it's okay', 'no problem', and 'never mind'.

괜찮아요 — the multi-use phrase

괜찮아요 literally means 'it's fine/okay'. Use it to reassure someone you're okay (after a stumble), to accept an offer you previously declined, to say 'no thank you', or to express 'that's acceptable'. Mastering its tone is key — same words, very different messages.
